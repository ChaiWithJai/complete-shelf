The founder’s arc

Dear stranger,

I made this because I got tired of using knowledge to stand one inch outside my own life. This is the story of the three forces I found underneath: anger, ambition, and tenderness.

Anger I Ambition II Tenderness III

Anger was the wound. Ambition became the armor. Tenderness taught me what strength was for.

Chapter I

Anger

What was it protecting?

Achilles gave me a language for a force I knew but did not know how to hold. His anger is immense because his wound is immense. He can bend a war around his refusal, but he cannot make himself invulnerable to grief.

The poem does not let us admire anger from a safe distance. It asks whom our anger punishes. It asks what it costs while we call it strength. Most painfully, it asks what softer truth the anger has been hired to protect.

Dear Dad, I kept calling the wound a question because questions sounded more intelligent than grief.note left after writing to you
No photograph here. The absence is the evidence.

My writing about my father belongs here. His absence left me angry at what I did not receive and afraid of repeating it. I could turn that hurt into analysis before I let myself feel it. Writing “Dear Dad” and “What is a father?” did not resolve the wound. It made the wound visible.

Under the anger was grief. Under the grief was a wish to be known, guided, and chosen. I had to decide what kind of presence I would offer when the person who should have taught me presence chose not to be there.

Homer’s outlier is not that he made Achilles impossibly strong. It is that he let the strongest man become reachable. An enemy father enters his shelter. They grieve in the same room. For a moment, recognition does what force could not.

That was the first turn. Anger did not have to become my identity. It could become evidence. If I could admit the tenderness beneath it without despising myself, I could stop passing the absence forward.

Chapter II

Ambition

When did achievement become armor?

In 2025 I had to admit that I could explain systems and help other people ship while failing to direct those skills toward my own life. I collected frameworks faster than I used them. I could build a complex system before proving that a simple one worked. I was better at designing a life than living one.

Ambition protected me too. As long as I was learning, building, or planning something impressive, I did not have to face the ordinary work I was avoiding. Philosophy could name an emotion before I felt it. A new tool could create motion without asking whether I was moving.

I was better at designing a life than living one. That sentence hurt because it was useful.the 2025 postmortem

I called my review a strategic postmortem because I needed evidence, not another story about how smart or broken I was. Professor Jiang gave me a clearer test: mass × energy × coordination.

MassWhat have I gathered?
EnergyWhat can I honestly bring?
CoordinationWhom does the work help move?

I already had mass: books, notes, code, skills, work experience, and people willing to help. I often mistook stored capacity for momentum. Energy meant the sleep, breath, health, grief, and meaning I could actually bring to that capacity. Coordination meant choosing what joined the book to the lesson, the lesson to a person, and the person to a next action.

Game theory made the final question impossible to avoid. Other people have goals, fears, beliefs, and choices of their own. Strategy is not arranging the world around my ambition. It is learning the situation well enough to create a move other people can enter.

Exceptionalism stopped meaning that I was privately special. It became a responsibility to develop what I have and direct it toward more than my own image.

Chapter III

Tenderness

What if care is the highest form of coordination?

No ships. No city walls. Just an alarm, wet ground, a friend waiting, and the quality of attention I bring into a room. The ordinary day became the place where every large idea had to prove itself.

Tenderness is not the opposite of rigor. It is what makes rigor answerable to a human life. Once I saw the gap in myself, an idea had to change what I did with a morning, how I cared for my body, how I treated a friend, or what a student could do next. Knowledge had to return me to life with better judgment.

That is why teaching became central. At KVibe Studios, I kept the workshops small because I did not want to perform expertise at a room. I wanted each person to ask several questions and leave with a clearer next move. Care was part of the method. So were real costs, failed demos, and the limits of what AI could do. If I could not show the receipt, I did not want to make the claim.

I taught through means, motivation, and opportunity. Means asks what a person already has. Motivation asks what change they want enough to work for. Opportunity asks for the smallest useful result they can make now. The first win is small on purpose. It lets a person feel evidence, not just receive advice.

6:00 a.m. The alarm is not a philosophy. Alex is waiting. Bring the wraps. Bring a plan. Be there.the park, before sunrise
6:04 a.m. No photograph. We were there to train.

I am training Alex for a fight on August 11. We meet at 6 a.m. in a local park. That detail belongs here because another person now feels the quality of how I direct myself. If I arrive tired or distracted, his session gets worse. Care requires preparation. Tenderness can look like wrapped hands, a plan, and being there before sunrise.

The park has changed for me too. It is where I train, notice where I live, and think without another screen in front of me. I wanted more time for imagination. I began to understand that imagination is not an escape from discipline. It depends on protected time, rest, health, and enough inner quiet to notice a future before it exists.

The Great Books hold these forces at human scale. Anger can reveal a wound or consume a world. Ambition can build a civilization or make another person into material. Tenderness can restore what power alone cannot.

I do not want this shelf to turn books into trophies or short summaries. I want each lecture to preserve Professor Jiang’s three questions and give the reader a place to continue. A question keeps the work open. It lets a person test an old argument against a current life.

This is what I mean when I call great books technologies of human possibility. Civilization becomes useful when inherited knowledge changes how I direct myself, how I meet another person, and what future we can coordinate together.

A small thing for the stranger who made it this far

You do not have to become impressive to become useful.

You can let the anger tell the truth without letting it choose the future. You can put the ambition down long enough to hear what it has been protecting. You can begin with one promise small enough to keep. Then keep it beside someone.

A letter

For Professor Jiang

Thank you. I remember your late December letter about turning 34 because it reached me when I needed to reorganize my life around imagination without giving up discipline or strategy. Your work gave me a standard for joining those things.

I am trying to become the kind of teacher who keeps learning in public and takes old knowledge seriously. I want to give that knowledge back in a form that helps another person move.

I am trying to build systems that give people more time to live. I am also testing whether my ideas remain useful during an ordinary morning, while caring for my body, while helping a friend, and while teaching a room of students.

I hope to share this shelf with you when you come to America, and especially New York. This project is my thank you.
