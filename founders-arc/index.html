Why I built this shelf

Anger, ambition, tenderness

Last June I wrote two essays in two days. The first was a letter to my father. The second was my attempt to answer the question that letter left me holding: what is a father? I got as far as protection, guidance, unconditional love — and then the part I did not see coming. The person who has to provide those things for me now is the man in the mirror.

I ended that essay with a practice I still use: “What is one thing I can do for myself today?”

This shelf is what happened when I kept answering that question for a year. Three forces kept showing up in the answers — anger, ambition, tenderness — and one teacher kept showing up in the middle of all three. This page is the story, and at the end of it, the thank-you letter I owe him.

Anger I Ambition II Tenderness III

Chapter I

Anger

What was it protecting?

The facts from the letter, plainly. My father is alive and chooses not to be present. I was the man of the house at three years old. I learned to mute my emotions before I was ten. At 34, I am now older than he was when I was born. And the ending that surprised me while I wrote it: I still love him. I love my mother, I love what remains, and I still love him.

For most of my life I did not call any of that anger. I called it drive. It looked like drive. It got promotions.

Then I sat with Professor Jiang’s lecture on the Iliad. His catalog question for it is one sentence: what assignment does Achilles receive? Achilles’ anger is enormous because his wound is enormous. He can bend an entire war around his refusal, and he still cannot protect himself from grief. Near the end of the poem an enemy father kneels in his tent and asks him to remember his own father — and grief does, in one night, what force could not do in ten years of war.

That lecture is why I can tell you what my anger was protecting. Grief. And under the grief, a wish so simple it embarrasses me to type it: to be known, guided, and chosen. The Iliad did not fix that. It gave the wound a language, and it gave me somewhere to put the weight that was not another person’s shoulders.

Chapter II

Ambition

When did achievement become armor?

In February I published the rawest thing I have ever written: I asked an AI to tell me what is wrong with me, and I posted the answer. 2025 was the worst year of my life — and no, it is not a pity party. The machine’s read on my pattern was rude and correct: high-achiever, wannabe founder, thinkboi. New ideas felt like completed work. I collected frameworks the way some people collect supplements. I used spirituality to bypass feelings I did not want to feel. And the answer was always in the one place I refused to look: the work I was avoiding.

That is the shape ambition took in me. Armor. As long as I was learning something impressive, I never had to ship something small.

Then, in late December, at the bottom of that year, Professor Jiang published a letter on his Substack called “Onward and Forward.” He tells the story of being thirty in his parents’ basement — angry, depressed, no prospects. Then four years of rise: two successful study-abroad programs, real prominence in education reform, a straight road to fame and fortune. And at the end of that rise — right around the age I am now — he walked away from all of it, because he no longer believed in what he was selling. The walk-away did not save him. He was poor again, depressed again; he drank; he wandered. What finally gave his talent a shape was not a better system. He says it in one line I have not stopped thinking about since December:

“My wife has made many sacrifices to give me structure so that I may direct my talent for good.”

Xueqin Jiang, “Onward and Forward,” December 2025

Love and responsibility did what optimization could not. Reading that at the bottom of my worst year reorganized it. If the fall comes after choosing what is true, the fall is survivable. That is what I needed a grown man to show me, and no framework ever had.

Three things I took from Professor Jiang now run through everything on this site.

Mass × energy × coordinationMass is what you have gathered. Energy is what you can honestly bring. Coordination is the multiplier that was missing from my life.
Game theoryOther people have goals, fears, and moves of their own. Strategy is reading the situation well enough to make a move others can enter.
ExceptionalismNot “I am special.” A responsibility: I was given real capacity, so develop it and point it at something bigger than my own reflection.

Here is what those meant when I finally used them on myself instead of putting them in a slide. I had mass — books, code, notes, skills, people willing to help — and I kept mistaking stored capacity for momentum. My energy was garbage: bad sleep, unprocessed grief, attention shredded by proving myself. And I had almost no coordination: nothing connected the book to the lesson, the lesson to a person, the person to a next step. So the work of this year was not another framework. It was raising the energy, and building the coordination. This shelf is one of the results.

Chapter III

Tenderness

What is strength for?

Teaching is the love of my life. I learned AI the only way you actually can — on the job, by breaking things. Six production outages. What survived them was not brilliance; it was postmortems, decision records, and workflows. So in January I turned all of it into a free guide, because the bartenders, baristas, friends, and family in my neighborhood deserve the same on-ramp the engineers got.

The workshops grew out of that, and I kept them small on purpose. I was not really teaching tools. I was teaching people to think in means, motivation, and opportunity — the other lens I carried out of Professor Jiang’s work. Means: what do you already have? Motivation: what change do you want badly enough to work for? Opportunity: what is the smallest useful thing you can make right now? The first win is small on purpose. Advice bounces off people. Evidence does not.

And then there is the park. Alex is a friend from the Hut, the bar I go to, and he has a fight on August 11. I train him at six in the morning. That fact carries more of my philosophy than anything else on this page: another person can now feel the quality of how I direct myself. If I arrive under-slept and distracted, his session gets worse. Tenderness turned out to be a discipline — wrapped hands, a plan, showing up before sunrise.

The same park is where I go to imagine. That is the redirect this whole year has been: less time proving I am smart, more hours protected for the thing I actually wanted — imagination. And imagination, it turns out, has prerequisites. Rest. Health. Quiet. Mornings that belong to me. Anger could not buy those. Ambition would not. Tenderness did.

So why Great Books?

Learning from civilization

Because I wanted to learn from civilization the way Professor Jiang does — as a working inheritance, not a trophy case. The man imbibes knowledge and turns it into teaching, and I have decided that is the kind of teacher I am becoming: not the guy with the frameworks, the guy whose students leave with evidence.

So this shelf preserves 25 of his recorded sessions: 13 Great Books lectures, each carrying the exact three questions he wrote for it — 39 questions, untouched — plus 12 seminar sessions walking through Dante. I did not summarize the lectures into content. A summary closes a book. A question keeps it open, and lets you test a three-thousand-year-old argument against the life you woke up in this morning.

Anger can reveal a wound or consume a world. Ambition can build a civilization or turn people into material. Tenderness restores what power alone cannot. The Great Books hold all three at human scale — which is why they are on this shelf, and why I am on this page.

A letter

Dear Professor Jiang,

Thank you. Your December letter reached me at the bottom of the worst year of my life, and it did something no framework had ever done for me: it showed me a man who walked away from a rising life because it stopped being true, fell hard a second time, and let love and responsibility — not optimization — give his talent its final shape. I stopped being so afraid of my own fall somewhere in the middle of reading it.

Everything I took from you is in use. Mass, energy, and coordination stopped being vocabulary and started being how I run my weeks. Game theory got me out of my own head and into rooms with actual people in them. And your way of taking in civilization — reading as inheritance, teaching as transmission — is the standard this shelf is reaching for. Your thirteen lectures are here with your thirty-nine questions exactly as you wrote them, because a student should meet your questions, not my summary of them.

I know you are coming to America — New York, specifically. Good. This is my city. When you get here, there is a classroom I teach in, a park where I train a friend at six in the morning, and a bar called the Hut where that friendship started. I would be honored to show you what your teaching built an ocean away from where you gave it.

The shelf is my thank-you. The welcome is real. — Jai

And for you

One small thing

If any of this feels like your own unfinished year, take the practice — it is the only advice on this page, and I did not invent it, I just kept answering it:

“What is one thing I can do for myself today?”

Answer it once, small, today. The shelf will be here when you want company from the last three thousand years.
